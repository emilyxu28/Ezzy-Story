The Distance Between the Front Door and the Kitchen

A community story featuring Ezzy Harrison

Ezzy Harrison was 21 years old, finishing her nutrition degree at Holyoke Community College, when the world shut down. She was living at home in Northampton with her mother and two brothers — one of them about to graduate high school. But even at home, Ezzy felt far away.

“To shift completely remote was, it was devastating… Even at home felt a little disconnected… It was definitely hard to really feel a community connection while we were on lockdown.”

— Ezzy Harrison

Ezzy is immunocompromised. Her mother was an essential worker, out of the house for long stretches. The family developed a protocol: if anyone came through the front door, Ezzy had to be somewhere else. There was a sanitation station. Everyone got cleaned up before they got close to her.

“It was really tough and endearing to see. On one hand, I felt really bad that everyone was so cautious. But on the other, we were just this team and a force to be reckoned with. We weren't gonna let it bring us down.”

— Ezzy Harrison

During lockdown, Ezzy and her family shared video tutorials of preserving strawberries online for her mother's clients and their community, a way to preserve strawberries and preserve some lightness in a heavy year.

“We all did it in real time. And we made a bunch of little jars that we were able to package up and ship out to people.”

— Ezzy Harrison

01

The Birthday That Felt Like Coming Home

The first time Ezzy felt community again after lockdown was on her 21st birthday. She was at Community Action's Gen Q program when she discovered something remarkably surprising: one of the other participants shared her exact birthday. Same day, same year, and they even share the same interest for baking.

“We were like, we're both gonna get a cake and bring it in on the same day and just celebrate… They had a family friend who made this really cool tiered cake, and I made a gluten and dairy free tres leches cake. Everyone was just sharing the cakes and like cutting up pieces on the same plate… And we all just sat together and talked. It was like the first time after the pandemic that I had community.”

— Ezzy Harrison

That pure moment — two strangers discovering they were birthday twins over shared cake — became a turning point. It also made Ezzy realize how reviving community could be: It’s sitting at a table with people who happened to love the same thing she did; It’s being brought back to life after so much isolation; It’s facing the emptiness the pandemic left behind yet choosing to connect anyway.

02

Food as Medicine, Food as Culture

Ezzy's relationship with food runs deep. She was diagnosed with Crohn's Disease as a teenager, which led her to study nutrition. But food was never just clinical for her.

“Food for me is way more than fuel. It's soul food. It's my culture. Just being able to make a dish that looks nice really will just boost my day and my mood.”

— Ezzy Harrison

Her favorite food shifts with her feelings. Sad days call for soups and stews, while happy days are sushi days. But when she's proud and celebrating, there's only one choice: her family's authentic Puerto Rican food.

“It's always been a part of who I am. It's always been the center of every family function. And it's always been the thing that brings us together.”

— Ezzy Harrison

During the pandemic, that togetherness was harder to share. Because Ezzy couldn't go to the grocery store herself, her mother went instead, navigating crowded aisles and strict time windows.

“There was a lot of structure in the grocery stores as well as having to show up at a certain time. And sometimes we were a little too late, or like the timing was off, and maybe the grocery store was a little too crowded… [Then] we weren't able to get that grocery at that time.”

— Ezzy Harrison

And when they relied on food pantries, Ezzy couldn't pick her own food. Because of strict health limitations, she can't eat gluten or dairy.

“Being able to pick out things that were gluten free was really difficult during a pandemic and there wasn't much access to that.”

— Ezzy Harrison

03

What She Saw at the Produce Prescription Program

Ezzy experienced these barriers in her own kitchen before she ever saw them at the Produce Prescription program. Interning with Healthy Hampshire's Produce Prescription program, which gives fresh produce to people who need it, she realized something worth noting about food accessibility.

“What I saw from the produce prescription, actually, was that when people have access to these foods, they will use it. They will get the fresh produce if they can… I think if they had the same access to fresh produce and Whole Foods, that they would choose that over something that most likely won't make them feel good if they have a lot of it.”

— Ezzy Harrison

But access isn't just about what's on the shelf. It's about the systems that decide who gets to shop, when, and how.

“The government structure that they had created, I think, made it really hard for low income communities and people with disabilities to even access the bare minimum of care. And if I had a magic wand, I would waive it so that those structures would actually be helpful.”

— Ezzy Harrison

Ezzy also saw something else: communities refusing to wait for systems to save them.

“What we often see is the community that is impacted, coming together and uplifting their own voices. And that's something that really needs to be done around nutrition and health.”

— Ezzy Harrison

04

“Growing” Forward

During the pandemic, Ezzy's family started growing their own food. She learned to grow parsley from home, which she deemed “a huge step” because she hadn't grown her own food before. She also worked at the Grow Foods Giving Garden in Florence, harvesting food, packing it into baskets, sending it out.

“It felt amazing. It was centering, it was grounding. It was just… I don't even know if I could find the right word for the feeling.”

— Ezzy Harrison

But self-sufficiency, she realized, isn't the whole answer.

“I feel it's a double-edged sword in a way because I think if people in your community need help, then you know we're all supposed to come together. I'd much rather that than being super sufficient by yourself. Which is why I want to be able to grow food and share it with other people.”

— Ezzy Harrison

05

Looking ahead — what Ezzy Wants the Future to Know

Ezzy is 23 now. The pandemic isn't over for her; physically, maybe, but not internally. She still sees the split in how people move through the world: some acknowledging that others are more at risk, others just wanting to get back to normal.

“I would love to leave behind the fear of insecurity and the anxiety of contaminating other people or getting contaminated… I'd love to just leave behind or create something that we can learn from.”

— Ezzy Harrison

She also developed a unique take on viewing the pandemic and isolation.

“I think confirmation, like saying it did happen. And you're not crazy for feeling the way that you're feeling or the way it impacted you, and that we're not going to move past it. We're going to take our time and heal.”

— Ezzy Harrison

And she has a message for whoever comes next.

“I would love for them to know that community and family are really going to be the ones to help you. I would love for them to know that their voices matter when things aren't just, and that they can, if they all come together, change these sort of systems that are in their way.”

— Ezzy Harrison

Ezzy Harrison is a nutrition student at Holyoke Community College and a former intern with Healthy Hampshire's Produce Prescription program.
This story was recorded in November 2023 as part of the Hampshire County Food Policy Council's community storytelling archive.
